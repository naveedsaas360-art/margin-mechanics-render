# EP01 — How Costco Actually Makes Money (It's Not the Groceries)
Target length: 9–10 min · ~1,400 words · Narration pace: calm, confident, slightly fast.

## 0:00 — HOOK (most important 15 seconds)
[VISUAL: visuals/01_hook.png, slow zoom-in]
Last year, Costco sold almost two hundred and seventy billion dollars' worth of stuff.
And here's the strange part — the stuff is not really where the profit comes from.

[VISUAL: visuals/02_fees_vs_profit.png]
Costco collected five point three billion dollars in membership fees. Its entire net profit for the year? About eight point one billion.
So a plastic card in your wallet is worth roughly two-thirds of everything Costco keeps.

Today we're opening up the machine.

## 0:25 — THE SETUP
[Stock: "warehouse store aisle"]
Most stores play a simple game: buy a product, mark it up, sell it, keep the difference.
Costco plays a different game. It wants to make as little as possible on the product... and then charge you for the right to walk in the door.

[VISUAL: visuals/03_two_models.png]
On the left, a normal retailer: profit from every item on the shelf.
On the right, Costco: products sold near cost, profit from the membership.

That one flip changes everything — the prices, the store layout, even the hot dogs. Let's go piece by piece.

## 1:15 — PART 1: THE MEMBERSHIP MACHINE
[VISUAL: visuals/04_fee_growth.png]
In fiscal 2025, membership fee income rose ten percent, from four point eight to five point three billion dollars.
Part of that came from a fee increase in September 2024: the basic Gold Star membership went from sixty to sixty-five dollars, and Executive went from one-twenty to one-thirty.

Now think about what a membership fee actually costs Costco to deliver.
The warehouse is already built. The staff are already paid. The lights are already on.
So when you pay sixty-five dollars, almost all of it drops straight toward profit. It's the closest thing in retail to free money.

And people keep paying.
[VISUAL: visuals/05_renewal.png]
In the US and Canada, ninety-two point three percent of members renew. Worldwide, it's about ninety percent.
For comparison, plenty of streaming services would celebrate keeping customers that well — and they don't ask you to drive to a warehouse.

[Re-hook — 2:30]
But why would anyone pay just to go shopping? The answer is the most disciplined pricing rule in retail.

## 2:40 — PART 2: THE MARKUP CAP
[Stock: "bulk groceries pallets"]
Costco has long been reported to cap its markups — around fourteen percent on branded goods and fifteen on its own Kirkland Signature products.
A typical supermarket can mark items up far more than that.

[VISUAL: visuals/06_markup.png]
This isn't generosity. It's strategy.
If prices are reliably low, the membership feels like it pays for itself. And if the membership pays for itself, you renew. And renewals are the profit.

So Costco will happily make almost nothing on a television — because the TV is just the reason you keep the card.

## 3:40 — PART 3: FEWER PRODUCTS, MORE POWER
[Stock: "shopping cart warehouse"]
Walk into a typical supermarket and you'll find tens of thousands of different products.
Costco carries roughly four thousand.

That sounds like a weakness. It's actually a weapon.
When Costco orders a product, it orders it in enormous volume, for hundreds of warehouses at once. That gives it huge bargaining power with suppliers.
Fewer products also means faster-moving inventory, simpler stores, and less waste.

[Re-hook — 4:20]
And when a supplier won't hit Costco's price? Costco just makes the product itself.

## 4:30 — PART 4: KIRKLAND SIGNATURE
[VISUAL: title card "KIRKLAND = COSTCO'S OWN BRAND" — make in Clipchamp, yellow text on dark]
Kirkland Signature is Costco's private label, and it's one of the biggest consumer brands in the world by sales.
Store brands give a retailer two things: a better margin than name brands, and leverage. If a big brand demands a higher price, Costco can put its own version right next to it.
Kirkland is also a loyalty engine — you can't buy most of it anywhere else. Want Kirkland? You need the card.

## 5:15 — PART 5: THE HOT DOG
[Stock: "hot dog" ]
And then there's the famous hot dog and soda combo: a dollar fifty, the same price since 1985.
Adjusted for inflation, that price should be several times higher today.

Costco keeps it at a dollar fifty anyway — because the hot dog isn't a product. It's a promise.
It tells every member: we will not squeeze you. And that promise is worth far more than the margin on a hot dog.

[Re-hook — 5:50]
So low prices, a cheap hot dog, a small product range... How does that add up to eight billion dollars of profit? Let's put the machine together.

## 6:00 — PART 6: THE FLYWHEEL
[VISUAL: visuals/07_flywheel.png — animate by zooming to each step]
Step one: low prices attract members.
Step two: more members means more buying power.
Step three: more buying power means even lower prices from suppliers.
Step four: lower prices make the membership more valuable — so people renew and new members join.
And every turn of that wheel adds more fee income at the top.

It's a loop that feeds itself. And the bigger Costco gets, the harder it is for anyone to copy.

## 7:00 — PART 7: WHY COMPETITORS CAN'T JUST COPY IT
[Stock: "empty retail store"]
On paper, any retailer could do this. In reality, it's brutal.
To run on razor-thin margins, you need massive scale from day one. You need members who trust you before you've earned it. And your shareholders have to accept that you'll deliberately leave money on the table on every sale.

Most companies simply can't stomach that. Costco has done it for decades, which is exactly why its customers believe the prices.

## 7:45 — PART 8: THE RISKS
[VISUAL: visuals/02_fees_vs_profit.png again, darker]
The model isn't invincible.
If renewal rates slip, the most profitable piece of the business shrinks fast.
Fee increases have to be rare — raise them too often and the "it pays for itself" feeling breaks.
And online shopping makes the warehouse trip less special every year.

But for now, the numbers say the machine is running stronger than ever.

## 8:20 — THE TAKEAWAY
[VISUAL: visuals/01_hook.png]
So, how does Costco make money?
It sells you groceries at almost no profit... so that you'll happily pay it every year for the privilege of buying them.
The store is the bait. The membership is the business.

## 8:40 — OUTRO / CTA
And Costco isn't the only company hiding its real business.
Airlines, for example, make billions not from flying — but from credit cards. That breakdown is next.
